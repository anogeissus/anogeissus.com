How the ApolloOne Focus Score Works

One number, from 1 to 100, for how well the camera focused where it was aimed. Here is what that number is measured from, where the work runs on your Mac, and what changed in Build 5582.

A customer sent me two frames of his dog, shot on a Z 9 at ISO 6400. Both came back at 100. One is a portrait that is good without being critically sharp, and the other is grossly out of focus. Working out why meant taking my own measurement apart, so this is a good moment to describe the whole of it in plain language.

What the number is measured from

Every RAW file records where the camera focused. ApolloOne reads that area out of the file and measures inside it, at the image's own full resolution. Nothing outside the focus area changes the score.

The measurement itself is one idea, repeated a few thousand times per photograph: how many pixels a boundary takes to go from dark to light.

Edge width

Photograph the edge of a roof against a bright sky twice, once focused and once not. In the focused frame the roof line goes from dark to bright in about two pixels. In the missed frame the same line smears across ten or fifteen. That distance is the edge width, and measuring it is the whole of the Focus Score.

A focus area holds thousands of edges, and most of them are not the finest thing in it. So ApolloOne sorts them by width and reads the sharpest quarter. If the best edges in the area are about two pixels wide, the camera got it. If the best edges in the area are seven pixels wide, nothing in that area is in focus.

That width is then put on a fixed scale. About two pixels is 100, and the number falls as the width grows. The scale does not depend on the rest of the folder, so the same photograph gets the same score anywhere, next to any other images.

Why the RAW is developed again

Every camera sharpens the picture it writes, and sharpening draws crisp edges around whatever detail is left. A frame the lens nearly missed comes out looking much like one it nailed. So ApolloOne does not judge a RAW file by the picture built into it (embedded JPEG). It develops the RAW itself, with sharpening off, for the focus area only. That costs a fraction of a second per image and it scores considerably harder: a frame that is perfectly useable but not critically sharp lands in the fifties where the camera's processed JPEG image would have put it in the nineties.

The rules around the edges

Three situations need their own handling.

  • Faces. Cameras smooth skin, and a face judged on skin alone reads softer than it is. When the focus area lands on a face, the file keeps the camera's processed JPEG image and is judged on the finest detail a face renders, the eyes and lashes and hair.
  • An empty focus area. Focus and recompose puts the recorded area on blank sky. When there is nothing in it to judge, ApolloOne looks for the subject in the frame, then searches the whole frame, and only calls the image Unranked when neither finds anything.
  • High ISO. Grain has hard little edges of its own, and above ISO 6400 what survives the camera's processing measures the same as fine detail. ApolloOne answers with a second developing of the same area, noise reduction at maximum: real detail survives that, grain does not. Above ISO 10000 even the second look can be fooled, and those images go to Unranked rather than get a guess.

Where the work runs

Processing a folder keeps all three engines busy, and the split is not the one people usually expect. The heavy, slow part is developing the RAW, which is graphics work. The measurement that produces the number is arithmetic over a small grey image, and it is quick enough that putting it anywhere but the CPU would cost more than it saved.

The parts that could use the Neural Engine already do, through Vision and through Apple's decoder. Nothing in the measurement itself is a neural network.

The two frames that broke it

Both were shot at ISO 6400 with subject detection set to animals. Both scored 100. The focus area the camera recorded on the second one landed on flat chest fur.

The right-hand crop is the interesting one. There is nothing in it. No detail of any kind survived the miss, and ApolloOne still reported edges two pixels wide.

What noise reduction leaves behind

The second look at high ISO works by turning noise reduction up to maximum and asking what is still there. On a frame of pure grain that check does its job and everything collapses. On this frame it did not.

The reason turned out to be a loop in my own measurement. Before ApolloOne measures any edges it estimates how noisy the area is, so that it can set its thresholds above the noise. That estimate looks at the finest, highest-frequency variation in the picture, which is exactly what noise reduction removes. Run noise reduction at maximum and the estimate drops through the floor: on this file it fell from 0.741 to 0.247. ApolloOne then concluded the area was very clean, relaxed its thresholds to match, and started accepting things it would otherwise have thrown away.

What it accepted were the leftovers of the noise reduction. Strong smoothing does not produce a flat surface, it produces soft blotches, and the boundary between two blotches is narrow. A narrow boundary is what the Focus Score counts as fine detail.

Every edge the measurement accepted, painted onto the two areas. Red marks the ones narrow enough to count as sharp. On the right they are isolated specks in an empty field, and those specks set the score.

Painting the accepted edges back onto the picture is what settled it for me. On the blurred frame they are single pixels, scattered, with nothing joining them up. On the eye they cluster in the dark part of the iris, where the noise is worst and the estimate describes it least well.

Two new tests

Neither test measures sharpness. Both ask whether there is enough in the area for a sharpness statistic to mean anything, and both are cheap because the measurement already knew the answer and was throwing it away.

How many edges are there

An in-focus focus area carries between 10 and 20 edges for every hundred pixels. The eye carried 2. The chest fur carried one edge for every thousand pixels. When the count is that low, the sharpest tenth of the edges is not a description of a subject, and ApolloOne now stops using it and reads the plain quarter instead. That alone takes the eye frame from 100 to 48, which is about what I would give it looking at it myself.

Do the edges line up

A real edge is a line. It continues: the pixel next to it is also on an edge, and so is the one after that. A speck of noise has nobody next to it. For every edge it accepts, ApolloOne now counts how many other accepted edges lie within three pixels.

Across 456 test files the separation is wide and there is nothing in the middle of it. In-focus areas have between 87% and 99% of their edges sitting on contours. Frames already ruled soft sit at 69% to 82%. The chest fur sits at 6%.

Reading further down the list

My first attempt at the fix declared such a frame unjudgeable and sent it to Unranked, and I threw that away. My reference folders of out-of-focus shots already score 1 to 5, and pushing them into Unranked would have been a step backwards: you delete a 3 at a glance, where an Unranked image has to be opened and looked at.

The way out was already in the data I had. Sort the widths of the blurred frame from narrowest to widest and the list is in two parts. The bottom half sits at 1.5 to 1.7 pixels, which is the noise-reduction residue. Above that it climbs steeply into the real blur, and by nine tenths of the way along the list it reads 13.6 pixels, the same figure the known out-of-focus reference frames give at their sharpest quarter.

So when the contour test says the area holds specks rather than a picture, the score is taken from the 90th percentile of the same measurements. Nothing new is computed, and the reading is only used once ApolloOne already knows what kind of population it is. On a sharp frame the 90th percentile is meaningless, which is why it is never consulted there.

The blurred frame goes from 100 to 6. The eye goes from 100 to 48. Both tests only ever apply to a result the second, noise-reduced developing produced, which is what keeps them away from every score that was already correct.

What moved

I checked the change against 456 files, covering three camera brands and every reference folder I keep: known out-of-focus sets, a high-ISO set with ground truth, a 200-frame travel folder, a swimming meet, a lunch table with faces. Of those, 384 produced a measurement, 18 are handled by the second developing at all, and 8 changed.

What the score still does not claim

It describes how well the camera did where it was aimed. Most of the time that is the same question as whether the subject is sharp, and it stops being the same question when the camera was aimed somewhere else. A folder shot with the focus area pinned to the middle of the frame, subject moving around it, will score the middle of the frame.

It also describes the file in front of it. A RAW exported through a converter that reconstructs detail while it denoises will score higher than the camera's own version of the same frame, and one that leaves the sharpening to you will score lower. Both readings are honest accounts of the file each converter made, and neither is a verdict on the converter.

It remains a measurement of edges. It has no opinion on whether the photograph is worth keeping for any other reason, and a folder of well-focused frames that are all of the wrong moment will score high all the way through.
